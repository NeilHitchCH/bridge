    Bridge Cheat Sheet for Beginners: 7 Opening and Response Rules
    New to bridge? These seven rules cover most of the hands you will pick up. They are the Standard American opening rules, and they are the heart of any beginner bridge bidding cheat sheet.
      Count your High Card Points: Ace = 4, King = 3, Queen = 2, Jack = 1.
      Open with 13 or more HCP. With 11–12, open only if the Rule of 20 says yes.
      Open a five-card major first. 1♥ or 1♠ always shows five or more cards.
      Open 1NT with 15–17 HCP and a balanced hand (no singleton, no void).
      No five-card major and not a 1NT hand? Open your longer minor: 1♣ with three cards in each minor, 1♦ with four in each.
      Open 2♣ with 22 or more HCP. It is artificial and forcing.
      When partner opens, respond with 6+ HCP, look for an eight-card major fit first, and bid game when your side has 25 combined points.

    Contract Bridge Basics: A 30-Second Rules Cheat Sheet
    Contract bridge is played by four people in two partnerships, North–South against East–West, and each player receives 13 cards. The auction decides the contract: how many tricks your side promises to win, and which suit, if any, is trumps.
      Contract = 6 + the level you bid. 1♠ promises 7 tricks, 3NT promises 9, 4♥ promises 10.
      Suit ranking: ♣ (lowest), ♦, ♥, ♠, then notrump (highest). Every bid must be higher than the one before it.
      The auction ends when a bid is followed by three passes in a row.
      Declarer is the player on the winning side who first named the final suit (or notrump). Their partner becomes dummy and lays their cards face up.
      Game bonuses come from 3NT, 4♥, 4♠, 5♣ and 5♦. That is why the point targets on this cheat sheet matter.
    For the full auction rules, see bridge bidding rules. For all the rules of play, read the bridge rules guide, and for points and bonuses, use the bridge scoring chart.

    How Do You Count Your Points in Bridge?
    Before you can bid anything, you need to know what your hand is worth. Bridge uses a system called High Card Points, or HCP for short. It was popularised in the 1920s and is still the foundation of every bidding system in the world today.

    Important: Do not add distribution points before you have found a fit with partner. A singleton is only an asset once you know your partner holds support for your long suit.
    How Many Points Do You Need to Open the Bidding?
    The general rule is 13 High Card Points to open at the one level. A hand of 12 points can be opened if it has a good long suit or strong distribution. The Rule of 20 is a useful shortcut for borderline hands: add your HCP to the length of your two longest suits. If the total is 20 or more, open.

    Standard American is a 5-card major system, which means an opening bid of 1♥ or 1♠ always promises at least five cards. So always open a five-card major if you have one. With two five-card suits, open the higher-ranking one. With no five-card suit, open your longer minor. If your minors are equal length, open 1♣.
    The 1NT opening is special. It is a very precise bid: exactly 15–17 points, no singletons, no voids. If your hand has a singleton anywhere, open a suit instead, even if you have 15 points.

    SAYC and 5-Card Major Cheat Sheet: The Core Agreements
    Standard American Yellow Card (SAYC) is the standard bidding system published by the American Contract Bridge League (ACBL), and it is the system this cheat sheet follows. It is a 5-card major system. If a partner or club asks “do you play SAYC?”, these are the agreements you are signing up for:
      5-card majors: 1♥ and 1♠ promise five or more cards, so partner can raise with three-card support.
      Strong 1NT: 15–17 HCP and a balanced hand.
      Strong 2♣: 22+ HCP, artificial and forcing.
      Weak two-bids: 2♦, 2♥ and 2♠ show a good six-card suit and 6–11 HCP.
      Core conventions: Stayman, Jacoby Transfers, Jacoby 2NT, Blackwood, Gerber over notrump openings, and takeout doubles.
    SAYC or 2 Over 1? 2 Over 1 Game Force also uses 5-card majors, but a new suit at the two level in response to a major opening (for example 1♠ – 2♣) commits the partnership to game. Learn SAYC first; most players add 2 Over 1 later.
    For the full system, read the guide to Standard American bidding (SAYC), and for why five-card majors work, see five-card majors in bridge.

    The golden rule of responding: Always try to find a major suit fit first. If you and partner together hold eight or more cards in hearts or spades, that major suit game (4♥ or 4♠) is almost always better than 3NT.
    Bridge Conventions Cheat Sheet: When to Use Stayman, Jacoby Transfers, Blackwood and Gerber
    These four conventions are the ones every beginner needs to know, and together they form the core of any bridge bidding conventions cheat sheet. Each has a specific trigger. Using them at the wrong time is as costly as not using them at all.
    Stayman Convention Cheat Sheet
    When: Partner opens 1NT. You have 8 or more points and at least one four-card major (hearts or spades).
    The bid: 2♣ (artificial: it says nothing about clubs; it asks “do you have a four-card major?”)
      Stayman Responses
      2♦
No four-card major
      2♥
Four or more hearts
      2♠
Four or more spades (denies four hearts; with both majors, bid 2♥ first)
    Important: Do not use Stayman with fewer than 8 points. With 0–7 points and a long major, use Jacoby Transfers instead. Using Stayman with a weak hand can leave you stranded in a 2♠ contract on a four-three fit, which is usually worse than staying in 1NT.
    Those three answers are your whole Stayman convention cheat sheet. For a full treatment, see the Stayman convention guide.
    Jacoby Transfer Cheat Sheet
    When: Partner opens 1NT. You have five or more cards in hearts or spades: at any point count from 0 upward.
      Jacoby Transfer Bids
      2♦
Partner bids 2♥
You have five or more hearts
      2♥
Partner bids 2♠
You have five or more spades
    Why make partner declare? Because the 1NT opener holds the stronger hand. If partner plays the contract, their strong hand is hidden from the opponents, which is a tactical advantage.
    After the transfer: with a weak hand (0–7 points), pass. With an invitational hand (8–9 points), bid 2NT. With a game-forcing hand (10+ points), bid 3NT or raise to game in the major.
    Keep those follow-ups in mind and you have a simple Jacoby transfer cheat sheet. Learn more at the Jacoby Transfers guide.

    What Do You Bid When the Opponents Open First?
    Defensive bidding: bidding after the opponents have already opened: follows its own rules. You are no longer just describing your hand to partner; you are also competing for the contract or making life difficult for the opponents.
      Defensive Bidding Actions
      1-level overcall

      2-level overcall

      1NT overcall
15–18 HCP

      Jump overcall
5–10 HCP

      Takeout double
12+ HCP
3-card support in all unbid suits
    The takeout double is the most important tool in defensive bidding. It says nothing about the suit the opponents opened. It says: I have a good hand, I am short in their suit, and I want you to pick the best contract for our side. Partner of the doubler must bid their best suit unless they are very strong (12+ HCP), in which case they can pass for penalties.

      North has 14 HCP, no five-card major and three cards in each minor, so opens the convenient minor: 1♣.
      South has 13 HCP and four spades. The first job as responder is to bid four-card majors up the line: South responds 1♠.
      North now knows South has four spades. North also has four spades. An eight-card fit has been found. With 14 HCP and four-card support, North raises to 2♠: a single raise showing 12–15 HCP.
      South adds up: North showed 12–15 HCP with the raise. South has 13. Together they hold 25–28 points. That is game territory. South bids 4♠ directly.
    The final contract is 4♠ played by South, who bid spades first. The partnership holds a combined 27 HCP and a solid eight-card spade fit. This is exactly the kind of hand a cheat sheet is built to help you recognise.
    Where Should You Go Next?
    A cheat sheet gives you the framework; the game fills in the detail. Once opening bids and simple responses feel natural, learn the responses to 1NT in full, because that auction has more moving parts than any other. If you play at a duplicate club, you will soon meet Roman Key Card Blackwood, the version of Blackwood that counts the king of trumps as a fifth “ace.”

  Frequently Asked Questions
  How many points do you need to open the bidding in bridge?+In Standard American bridge, you need at least 13 High Card Points to open the bidding at the one level. You may open a hand of 11 or 12 points if your hand passes the Rule of 20: count your HCP and add the length of your two longest suits. If the total is 20 or more, opening is justified. The rule exists to prevent players from opening very weak hands while giving them the flexibility to open distributional hands with slightly fewer high cards.
How many points do you need for game in bridge?+You and your partner need a combined total of approximately 25 High Card Points to bid and make a game contract. In practice, 3NT and 4♥ or 4♠ are the most commonly bid games and both require around 25 combined points with an appropriate fit. Minor-suit games (5♣ or 5♦) require 28–29 combined points and are rarely bid unless a notrump game is unavailable. A small slam requires 33 combined points, and a grand slam requires 36–37 combined points plus all four aces.
What is the difference between Stayman and Jacoby Transfers in bridge?+Stayman and Jacoby Transfers are both used when partner opens 1NT, but they serve different purposes. Stayman (the 2♣ bid) asks whether partner has a four-card major suit, and requires 8 or more HCP to use. Jacoby Transfers (2♦ to show hearts, 2♥ to show spades) show a five-card major in your own hand and can be used at any point count, even with a weak hand you want to rescue from 1NT. Stayman searches for a four-four fit; transfers guarantee a five-card suit.
What is a limit raise in bridge and when should you use it?+A limit raise is when you raise partner’s opening major suit bid by two levels, for example from 1♥ to 3♥. It shows 10–12 High Card Points and at least three-card support for partner’s suit. The word “limit” describes the fact that it is not forcing: opener can pass with a minimum hand. It is the right bid when you have a fit and a hand strong enough to invite game, but not quite enough to bid game directly. With 13 or more points and four-card support, use Jacoby 2NT instead. Be careful not to jump straight to game (1♥ to 4♥) with a strong hand: in Standard American that jump shows a weak hand with five or more trumps.
Why do you open 1♣ or 1♦ with only three cards in the suit?+In Standard American, if your hand is not strong enough for 1NT (15–17 balanced) and you have no five-card major, you must open a minor suit even if your minor suits are short. This is called opening a “convenient minor” or “prepared minor.” It simply tells partner you have an opening hand without promising a long minor. The auction is designed to continue from there, with both players looking for a major suit fit or notrump. Opening 1♣ with a three-card suit is not a mistake; it is the correct Standard American opening with a balanced 13-point hand and no five-card major.

What is SAYC in bridge?+SAYC stands for Standard American Yellow Card, the standard bidding system published by the American Contract Bridge League (ACBL).
